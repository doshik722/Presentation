#TITLE: Information Security: Keeping Data Safe
#TEXT: What information security means, what dangers we face online, and simple habits that protect our data every day.
#IMAGE: laptop with a glowing padlock icon on the screen
#NOTES: Hello everyone. Today I want to talk about information security. Every day we use phones, computers and the internet, and every day our data can be in danger. In the next few minutes you will learn what the main dangers are and how to protect yourself.
#SLIDE
#TITLE: What Is Information Security?
#TEXT: Information security protects data from people who should not see it, change it or delete it. It covers computers, phones, paper files and, most importantly, people.
#DIAGRAM: type=tree; Information security > Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability; Confidentiality > Only the right people see data; Integrity > Data stays correct; Availability > Data is there when needed
#IMAGE: server room with rows of computer servers
#NOTES: Experts describe information security with three simple ideas. First, secret data must stay secret. Second, nobody should change data without permission. Third, data must be ready when we need it. For example, a hospital needs patient records to be private, correct and available at any time.
#SLIDE
#TITLE: Why It Matters
#TEXT: Most attacks do not start with clever hacking. They start with a human mistake: a click on a bad link, a weak password or a file sent to the wrong person.
#CHART: type=bar; title=Data breaches with a human element, % (Verizon DBIR report); x=2023,2024,2025,2026; y=74,68,60,62
#NOTES: Every year the company Verizon studies thousands of real data breaches. In its 2026 report, people played a role in 62 percent of them. So most problems are not about computers at all. This is good news, because people can learn to be careful.
#SLIDE
#TITLE: Common Threats
#BULLET: Phishing: fake messages that steal passwords
#BULLET: Malware: harmful programs hidden in files and links
#BULLET: Ransomware: locks your files and asks for money
#BULLET: Password attacks: guessing or stealing passwords
#IMAGE: computer screen showing a red warning message about a virus
#NOTES: Let me explain these threats in simple words. Criminals send fake emails, hide bad programs in downloads, or try thousands of passwords very quickly. Ransomware is especially dangerous: in the 2025 Verizon report it was found in 44 percent of breaches. One wrong click can stop the work of a whole company.
#SLIDE
#TITLE: How to Spot Phishing
#BULLET: The message is urgent or scary
#BULLET: The sender's address looks strange
#BULLET: The link goes to a different website
#BULLET: Someone asks for a password or money
#DIAGRAM: type=flow; Strange message -->|do not click| Check the sender -->|still not sure| Report to IT
#IMAGE: suspicious email on a laptop screen asking to confirm a bank password
#NOTES: Phishing is like fishing: the criminal throws bait and waits. A typical message says: your account is blocked, click here now. Before you click, stop and think for a few seconds. Real banks and real companies never ask for your password by email.
#SLIDE
#TITLE: Strong Passwords and Two Steps
#TEXT: A long password is much harder to guess than a short one. Each extra character multiplies the number of possible passwords.
#FORMULA: N = k^L
#FORMULA: 26^8 \approx 2 \times 10^{11} \qquad 94^{12} \approx 4.8 \times 10^{23}
#DIAGRAM: type=flow; Password -->|something you know| Code on your phone -->|something you have| Access granted
#NOTES: In this formula, k is the number of different symbols and L is the length of the password. Eight small letters give about two hundred billion variants, and a computer can try them very fast. Twelve characters with letters, numbers and symbols give a trillion times more. And with a second step, like a code on your phone, a stolen password alone is not enough.
#SLIDE
#TITLE: Updates and Backups
#TEXT: Updates close security holes that criminals already know about, so install them soon. Backups save you when something goes wrong, even after a ransomware attack.
#DIAGRAM: type=tree; The 3-2-1 backup rule > 3 copies of your data, 2 different types of storage, 1 copy in another place
#IMAGE: external hard drive connected to a laptop for a backup
#NOTES: Many people click remind me later when they see an update, but that is risky. A backup is a copy of your files. The 3-2-1 rule is easy to remember: three copies, on two kinds of storage, and one copy far away, for example in the cloud.
#SLIDE
#TITLE: Safe Habits Every Day
#BULLET: Lock your screen when you leave your desk
#BULLET: Be careful with free public Wi-Fi
#BULLET: Do not put secret work data into public AI chat tools
#BULLET: Think before you share personal details online
#IMAGE: person working on a laptop in a café with public Wi-Fi
#NOTES: Small habits make a big difference. It takes one second to lock your computer, but it stops anyone from reading your emails. Remember that anything you type into a public AI tool may be stored outside your company. And personal details on social media can help criminals write more believable fake messages.
#SLIDE
#TITLE: Security Is Everyone's Job
#TEXT: Technology helps, but people make the biggest difference. Learn the risks, build good habits and help others do the same.
#QUOTE: Security is a process, not a product. — Bruce Schneier, «Secrets and Lies»
#DIAGRAM: type=flow; Know the risks -->|learn| Good habits -->|every day| Safe data
#NOTES: Bruce Schneier is a famous security expert, and his idea is simple: you cannot buy security once and forget about it. It is something we do every day. Today, please check one thing: does your main account have a second step? Thank you for listening.
